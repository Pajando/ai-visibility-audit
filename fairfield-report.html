The State of AI Visibility in Fairfield: we examined 43 local businesses

By Alejandro Ojeda · The AO Audit, Napa CA · Scans run July 28, 2026, plus a targeted lawyer/eye-care sweep run August 15, 2026 · Aggregates only — no individual scores published

First, the problem in plain words: the people of Fairfield — 120,000 of them, the biggest city in Solano County — have started asking AI: "who can fix my AC today?" "How much is a haircut in Fairfield?" "Where's the best pho?" And AI doesn't answer with a page of ten blue links the way old Google did. It answers with one or two names. Most local businesses aren't in those answers — not because they're worse, but because their websites never told the machines what machines need to hear. That's invisibility by configuration, not by choice — and every gap this report found is fixable.

The one-sentence finding: the county seat still hides its prices — of the 31 readable Fairfield sites we've now scanned, 24 publish no visible prices a machine could quote (77% — roughly three of four businesses), and 10 of the 43 businesses we examined have no website at all (23%). The doors are open — only 2 sites turned our reader away, the machines walk right in — and find shelves with no price tags and, at most counters, no ID card.

Method, briefly: 43 currently-operating, locally-owned Fairfield businesses — 33 with real websites across restaurants, salons and barbershops, trades, auto shops, medical and dental, retail, coffee, fitness, veterinary care, professional offices, law firms, eye-care practices, and two Suisun Valley wineries (27 scanned July 28, 2026 plus 6 more — 3 lawyers, 3 eye-care practices — added August 15, 2026 in a targeted follow-up sweep for categories the original roster under-covered; 31 of the 33 were readable, 2 rejected the connection outright) plus 10 established, well-reviewed businesses for which repeated searching found no owned website at all. Roster notes for transparency: the original target was 30 websites and we published 27, because Fairfield kept handing us businesses that weren't Fairfield's — several strong candidates turned out to be headquartered in Vacaville, Vallejo, Benicia, or Suisun City (Suisun City is deliberately saved as a future town of its own; every address was checked against Fairfield's 94533/94534 zips), and two lookalikes were caught only by reading their actual pages: a "Fairfield" bike shop that lives in Fairfield, British Columbia, and a "Fairfield" restaurant in Fairfield, Ohio. Chains were excluded aggressively — the county seat is full of them — including Jelly Belly's corporate headquarters. One disclosure: one furniture retailer in the sample is part of a small California family group of three stores, included as family-owned and independent, with the multi-location fact disclosed rather than hidden. We publish aggregates only. Honest limit: a block against our reader proves a site rejects automated visitors like ours; individual AI crawlers may be treated differently by security systems — we report what we measured, not more.

First, meet the city we scanned

Fairfield is where Solano County does business — the county seat, the courthouse, the crossroads between the Bay and Sacramento. The roster we scanned reads like the city itself: a Jalisco-style Mexican kitchen (La Costa Azul), a Salvadoran-Mexican restaurant (Jalapeños), a Filipino kitchen (Katna's Kitchen), a Vietnamese pho house (SaiGon Nho), a Thai restaurant (Million Thai), barbershops and salons, roofers and electricians, and a tax office that works in two languages (Rosales Tax). And Fairfield holds a surprise the other three towns would recognize: its own wine country. The Suisun Valley AVA reaches inside the city's own 94534 zip code — the Lanza family has farmed Wooden Valley Winery there since 1955, and the Howard family pours at Tolenas. Fairfield doesn't need to borrow Napa's story; it has one. This report treats every business in it the same way: aggregates only, no individual scores published, and leaders named with respect.

Finding 1 — the county seat still hides its prices

This is the headline, and it's still true after the August sweep: roughly three of four Fairfield businesses show no prices a machine could quote — 24 of the 31 readable sites (77%), level with St. Helena's 77% and not far off the rest of the series (each report's own current figures: Napa 73%, American Canyon 60%, St. Helena 77%, Vacaville 76%, Calistoga 86% — the highest we've measured — and Yountville 64%). When a neighbor asks an AI "how much does a haircut cost in Fairfield?" or "what does a service call run around here?", the machine can only repeat prices that exist somewhere as readable text — and in Fairfield, they mostly don't. An honest nuance: for the trades, and for the lawyers added in August, "call for a quote" or "call for a consultation" is an old industry habit and sometimes a rational one, because jobs and cases vary. But a starting price, a service-call fee, a menu with numbers on it — anything quotable — is the difference between being the answer and being a maybe. Seven sites in the sample do publish prices, so it isn't foreign to Fairfield; it's just rare. Silence about price doesn't read as classy to a machine. It reads as nothing.

Finding 2 — a big city with a persistent no-website gap

Fairfield remains one of the largest cities we've examined — only Vacaville's 48-business roster is bigger now — and 23% of the businesses we examined (10 of 43) still have no website at all, against the figures our other reports currently publish: 18% in Napa, 25% in American Canyon (the highest of the series), 17% in St. Helena, 21% in Vacaville, 19% in Calistoga, and 19% in Yountville. These aren't marginal businesses. The ten include a barbershop open seven days a week, a family HVAC contractor in business since 2008 with an active state license, a family roofing company in business since 1989, a western-wear store, and a Mexican grocery — established, reviewed, operating. And the icon of the ten is beloved: The Jelly Donut on West Texas Street — established 1987, Fairfield's first and longest-running donut shop, called the best in Solano County nine years running in third-party write-ups. Humans have no trouble finding it. Machines find it only through review platforms and delivery apps — there is no website of its own for an AI to read, verify, or quote. If any business proves that being loved by people and being visible to machines are two entirely different things, it's the donut shop that's been winning for four decades without a single page of its own. That's not a criticism — it's the clearest picture we've found of where local commerce actually stands.

Finding 3 — open doors, empty ID cards (and one genuine surprise)

Here's what still makes Fairfield different from most of the towns in the series: the sites that exist mostly work. Only 2 of 33 sites turned our automated reader away — St. Helena turned away 7 of 29 — and only 2 of the 31 readable sites served near-blank pages. Fairfield's websites are workmanlike: they load, they describe themselves (74% have a meta description), they let the machines in. But once inside, the machines find 65% carrying no LocalBusiness structured data (20 of 31) — the machine-readable ID card that lets an AI confirm a business exists, where it is, and when it's open. Readable but unverifiable means an AI can look at you and still not be able to vouch for you. A more honest note added in August: two of the three law firms added to the roster, Barber Tubis Law and Hillman, Lucas & Canning, carry schema and llms.txt both — law offices out-configuring some of the salons. Three readable sites in the original sample actively block six named AI crawlers in their robots.txt — a setting an owner should get to decide on purpose, not inherit from a template. And the genuine surprise still holds: 39% of Fairfield's readable sites carry an llms.txt AI-instructions file (12 of 31) — behind only American Canyon's 45%, still one of the two best adoption rates we've measured, ahead of Napa's current 27% and St. Helena's 23%. Somebody in this county is paying attention.

Finding 4 — four towns, four different problems

This is our fourth town and our first outside Napa County, and each one fails differently — which is itself the lesson: there is no one small-city problem, there's only your configuration.

Napa's problem was the bouncer at the door. American Canyon's was having no building. St. Helena's was magnificent buildings machines can't see inside. Fairfield's is an open store with the lights on, no price tags on the shelves, and no ID at the counter — and, more than anywhere else we've been, whole businesses with no store on the machine's map at all.

Finding 5 — the bright spots (named with respect)

Nine businesses carry proper LocalBusiness-type structured data alongside an llms.txt AI-instructions file: La Costa Azul, Prestige Barbershop, Straight or Knot Salon, Freedom Dental, SaiGon Nho, Highflier Coffee, Zodiac Hair Studio, Barber Tubis Law, and Hillman, Lucas & Canning. Four more carry llms.txt as well: Dooley's Heating and Air, Michelle's Furniture & Mattress, Cal West Pet Hospital, and Rosales Tax. There's something worth pausing on in that first list: a Jalisco-style Mexican kitchen and a Vietnamese pho house sit on the double list alongside the salons, the dentist, and now two family-law and estate-planning firms — machine visibility in Fairfield already speaks more than one language and more than one profession. And the most complete profile in the whole sample still belongs to Straight or Knot Salon — structured data, visible prices, an AI-instructions file, a meta description, a sitemap, and more readable text than any other site we scanned, including the newly-added law firms. A neighborhood hair salon currently sets the technical standard for the biggest city in Solano County — which means the standard is real, local, and reachable. (We name only the leaders. Nobody gets called out here; every business in the sample can request its own private result, free.)

What this means if you own a business in Fairfield

Between the businesses with no website, the sites that hide every price, and the readable-but-unverifiable rest, most of Fairfield's independents can't currently be read, verified, and quoted by the machines — and the machines are now the phone book, the price-checker, and the first opinion for 120,000 neighbors. The fixes, in leverage order: if you have no website, one honest page is enough to start · publish your prices — even a starting price — as real text · install LocalBusiness schema · add an llms.txt file · check what your robots.txt tells AI crawlers, on purpose · keep your profiles agreeing everywhere.
