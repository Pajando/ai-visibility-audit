The State of AI Visibility in Fairfield: we examined 37 local businesses

By Alejandro Ojeda · The AO Audit, Napa CA · Scans run July 28, 2026 · Aggregates only — no individual scores published

First, the problem in plain words: the people of Fairfield — 120,000 of them, the biggest city in Solano County — have started asking AI: "who can fix my AC today?" "How much is a haircut in Fairfield?" "Where's the best pho?" And AI doesn't answer with a page of ten blue links the way old Google did. It answers with one or two names. Most local businesses aren't in those answers — not because they're worse, but because their websites never told the machines what machines need to hear. That's invisibility by configuration, not by choice — and every gap this report found is fixable.

The one-sentence finding: the county seat hides its prices — of the 25 readable Fairfield sites we scanned, 20 publish no visible prices a machine could quote (80% — four of five businesses), and 10 of the 37 businesses we examined have no website at all (27%) — both the highest we've measured in four towns. The doors are open — only 2 sites turned our reader away, the machines walk right in — and find shelves with no price tags and, at most counters, no ID card.

Method, briefly: 37 currently-operating, locally-owned Fairfield businesses — 27 with real websites across restaurants, salons and barbershops, trades, auto shops, medical and dental, retail, coffee, fitness, veterinary care, professional offices, and two Suisun Valley wineries (all scanned July 28, 2026 with our own automated checker; 25 were readable, 2 rejected the connection outright) plus 10 established, well-reviewed businesses for which repeated searching found no owned website at all. Roster notes for transparency: the target was 30 websites and we published 27, because Fairfield kept handing us businesses that weren't Fairfield's — several strong candidates turned out to be headquartered in Vacaville, Vallejo, Benicia, or Suisun City (Suisun City is deliberately saved as a future town of its own; every address was checked against Fairfield's 94533/94534 zips), and two lookalikes were caught only by reading their actual pages: a "Fairfield" bike shop that lives in Fairfield, British Columbia, and a "Fairfield" restaurant in Fairfield, Ohio. First, meet the city we scanned

Fairfield is where Solano County does business — the county seat, the courthouse, the crossroads between the Bay and Sacramento. The roster we scanned reads like the city itself: a Jalisco-style Mexican kitchen (La Costa Azul), a Salvadoran-Mexican restaurant (Jalapeños), a Filipino kitchen (Katna's Kitchen), a Vietnamese pho house (SaiGon Nho), a Thai restaurant (Million Thai), barbershops and salons, roofers and electricians, and a tax office that works in two languages (Rosales Tax). And Fairfield holds a surprise the other three towns would recognize: its own wine country. The Suisun Valley AVA reaches inside the city's own 94534 zip code — the Lanza family has farmed Wooden Valley Winery there since 1955, and the Howard family pours at Tolenas. Fairfield doesn't need to borrow Napa's story; it has one. This report treats every business in it the same way: aggregates only, no individual scores published, and leaders named with respect.

Finding 1 — the county seat hides its prices

This is the headline, and it's simple: four of five Fairfield businesses show no prices a machine could quote — 20 of the 25 readable sites (80%), the highest hidden-price rate of the four towns we've measured (each report's own figures: Napa 74%, St. Helena 77%, American Canyon 60%). When a neighbor asks an AI "how much does a haircut cost in Fairfield?" or "what does a service call run around here?", the machine can only repeat prices that exist somewhere as readable text — and in Fairfield, they mostly don't. An honest nuance: for the trades, "call for a quote" is an old industry habit and sometimes a rational one, because jobs vary. But a starting price, a service-call fee, a menu with numbers on it — anything quotable — is the difference between being the answer and being a maybe. Five sites in the sample do publish prices, so it isn't foreign to Fairfield; it's just rare. Silence about price doesn't read as classy to a machine. It reads as nothing.

Finding 2 — the biggest city has the most missing websites

Fairfield is by far the largest city we've examined, and it posted the highest no-website rate of the four: 27% of the businesses we examined (10 of 37) have no website at all, against the figures our earlier reports published: 19% in Napa, 25% in American Canyon, 17% in St. Helena. These aren't marginal businesses. The ten include a barbershop open seven days a week, a family HVAC contractor in business since 2008 with an active state license, a family roofing company in business since 1989, a western-wear store, and a Mexican grocery — established, reviewed, operating. And the icon of the ten is beloved: The Jelly Donut on West Texas Street — established 1987, Fairfield's first and longest-running donut shop, called the best in Solano County nine years running in third-party write-ups. Humans have no trouble finding it. Machines find it only through review platforms and delivery apps — there is no website of its own for an AI to read, verify, or quote. If any business proves that being loved by people and being visible to machines are two entirely different things, it's the donut shop that's been winning for four decades without a single page of its own. That's not a criticism — it's the clearest picture we've found of where local commerce actually stands.

Finding 3 — open doors, empty ID cards (and one genuine surprise)

Here's what makes Fairfield different from the towns before it: the sites that exist mostly work. Only 2 of 27 sites turned our automated reader away — St. Helena turned away 7 of 29 — and only 2 of the 25 readable sites served near-blank pages. Fairfield's websites are workmanlike: they load, they describe themselves (72% have a meta description — the best rate of our four towns' published figures), they let the machines in. But once inside, the machines find 72% carrying no LocalBusiness structured data (18 of 25) — the machine-readable ID card that lets an AI confirm a business exists, where it is, and when it's open. Readable but unverifiable means an AI can look at you and still not be able to vouch for you. Two more honest notes: three readable sites in the sample actively block six named AI crawlers in their robots.txt — a setting an owner should get to decide on purpose, not inherit from a template. And the genuine surprise: 44% of Fairfield's readable sites carry an llms.txt AI-instructions file (11 of 25) — alongside American Canyon's published 45%, one of the two best adoption rates we've measured, far ahead of Napa and St. Helena's published 22% and 23%. Somebody in this county is paying attention.

Napa's problem was the bouncer at the door. American Canyon's was having no building. St. Helena's was magnificent buildings machines can't see inside. Fairfield's is an open store with the lights on, no price tags on the shelves, and no ID at the counter — and, more than anywhere else we've been, whole businesses with no store on the machine's map at all.

Finding 5 — the bright spots (named with respect)

Seven businesses carry proper LocalBusiness-type structured data — and every one of the seven also carries an llms.txt AI-instructions file: La Costa Azul, Prestige Barbershop, Straight or Knot Salon, Freedom Dental, SaiGon Nho, Highflier Coffee, and Zodiac Hair Studio. Four more carry llms.txt as well: Dooley's Heating and Air, Michelle's Furniture & Mattress, Cal West Pet Hospital, and Rosales Tax. There's something worth pausing on in that first list: a Jalisco-style Mexican kitchen and a Vietnamese pho house sit on the double list alongside the salons and the dentist — machine visibility in Fairfield already speaks more than one language. And the most complete profile in the whole sample belongs to Straight or Knot Salon — structured data, visible prices, an AI-instructions file, a meta description, a sitemap, and more readable text than any other site we scanned. A neighborhood hair salon currently sets the technical standard for the biggest city in Solano County — which means the standard is real, local, and reachable. (We name only the leaders. Nobody gets called out here; every business in the sample can request its own private result, free.)

What this means if you own a business in Fairfield

Between the businesses with no website, the sites that hide every price, and the readable-but-unverifiable rest, most of Fairfield's independents can't currently be read, verified, and quoted by the machines — and the machines are now the phone book, the price-checker, and the first opinion for 120,000 neighbors. The fixes, in leverage order: if you have no website, one honest page is enough to start · publish your prices — even a starting price — as real text · install LocalBusiness schema · add an llms.txt file · check what your robots.txt tells AI crawlers, on purpose · keep your profiles agreeing everywhere.
