We inspected all 86 of our own URLs

Google crawled your page and still left it out of search? It did that to 12 of our Spanish pages.

By Alejandro Ojeda · The AO Audit · August 20, 2026 · Unless another date is given, every number below came from Google's own API this morning.

This is a report on crawled - currently not indexed, written from our own site's data, not theory. Getting a page stored by Google comes before everything else, including the work the industry calls AEO or GEO. We call it Answer Optimization (AO). A page Google didn't keep can't be shown as a link in Google's AI features either: Google says a page must be "indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet."

On August 20, 2026, our site was built in matched pairs. Every English page had a Spanish twin: 43 and 43, 86 pages total. That was a marketing decision, not an experiment. But it made a fairly clean one, because both halves share a template, a domain, a sitemap and a publishing date range. This morning we ran all 86 URLs through the Search Console API and read the verdict on every one.

What did the sweep actually find?

English: 36 of 43 pages indexed. Spanish: 23 of 43. Across the whole site, 59 of 86, which is a little over two thirds.

That much we half expected. We have known since our first full indexing sweep on August 15 that the Spanish side was lagging. What we did not expect was where the gap lives.

Is the problem finding the pages, or keeping them?

Keeping them. This is the whole story, and it surprised us.

Search Console gives many reasons a page isn't indexed, from "URL marked 'noindex'" to "Not found (404)" to duplicate and redirect reasons (Google's full list). On our site, the missing pages fell into three groups. Unknown: Google has never heard of the URL. Discovered but not crawled: Google knows the URL and hasn't fetched it yet. Or crawled and then left out, which shows up as crawled - currently not indexed. The first two are reach problems. The third happens after Google has already read the page.

Look at the bottom two rows. Pages Google never fetched: seven English, eight Spanish. Almost identical. Google's crawler reached our Spanish pages nearly as often as our English ones (35 vs 36). Discovery is not the problem, and our sitemap is not broken for one language.

Now look at the middle row. Twelve Spanish pages were fetched and then turned away. Zero English pages were. Not one.

Put it as a keep rate and it gets starker. Of the English pages Google actually crawled, it kept 36 of 36. Of the Spanish pages it actually crawled, it kept 23 of 35. Same template, same domain, same sitemap, same morning's check.

Google found our Spanish pages almost as easily as the English ones. It just didn't keep as many. Of the 13-page gap between 36 and 23, 12 pages sit in a decision Google makes after it has already read the page.

So why does Google decline them?

We don't know, and this is the part where most articles would hand you a confident reason. We measured what happened. We cannot prove why.

Our three best guesses, in the order we'd bet on them. One: a translated page on a young site may look too close to a page Google already stored. (Google's multi-language guide talks about duplicate content as a same-language issue, so this guess may be wrong.) Two: almost nothing on the open web links to our Spanish pages, so they arrive with no outside support. Three: Google may spend less effort on the version it thinks fewer people need. Google doesn't publish a rule that says any of that. If someone tells you exactly why your page was left out, they're guessing too. They're just not telling you.

One more honest limit: this is one site, ours, with 86 pages and not much authority yet. It is a real measurement, not a law of the internet.

What went wrong on our side?

Two things, and both are worse than the language gap.

First, the article we published three days ago about how Siri picks businesses is still, as of this morning, unknown to Google. Not declined. Not queued. Unknown, in both English and Spanish. We wrote a piece about being findable and Google does not yet know it exists.

Second, and this one stings: an article we published on July 19 has never been crawled. Not once, in 32 days. It sits in our sitemap. It's linked from our homepage footer and from the bottom of other articles Google visits regularly. None of that was enough. Google says a sitemap "doesn't guarantee that all the items in your sitemap will be crawled and indexed." We had quietly been treating it as a promise.

Is "not indexed" permanent?

No, and this is the useful part. On August 17 we had 17 pages sitting in the declined bucket. This morning, five of them are indexed. Google re-crawled them on August 18 and changed its mind. Four of those five were Spanish.

The word doing the work in that status is currently. Google says a page in this status "may or may not be indexed in the future" and there's "no need to resubmit this URL for crawling" (Google's help page). So don't delete the page or assume it's cursed. Give Google a reason to keep it: make the page more useful and link to it from pages Google already crawls. Google says to ask for a recrawl only after an important change.

What would we tell a business with this problem?

Open Search Console and inspect your own URLs one at a time. A live page is not the same as a found page. My take: the gap between them is where a lot of invisible businesses live.

Then read which bucket you're in, because the fix is different for each. Never crawled means work on links and requesting indexing. Crawled and declined means the page needs to be more worth keeping than the thing Google already has. And if you publish in two languages, check both halves separately. Nobody would have caught our 12 to 0 split by looking at a single site-wide number.

Why publish a number that makes us look bad?

Because 23 of 43 is our real number, and we sell honesty about numbers. We run a tool that checks how readable other people's websites are. A tool like that has no business hiding its own scoreboard, and the failures teach more than the wins. Our last few reports from these logs were a failing self-audit, a schema error inside our own schema checker, and a warning we left in place on purpose. This one is about a third of our own site (27 of 86 pages) missing from Google.

We will re-run this exact sweep next week and publish whether the 12 moved.

FAQ

What does crawled - currently not indexed mean?

Google's own description: "The page was crawled by Google but not indexed. It may or may not be indexed in the future; no need to resubmit this URL for crawling." So the page is reachable and nothing is blocking it. My take: Google decided not to store it, at least for now, and there's no error to fix. On our own site this status hit 12 Spanish pages and zero English pages on the same day.

Is crawled - currently not indexed permanent?

Not necessarily. On August 17, 2026 our site had 17 pages in that status. Three days later, five of them were indexed after Google re-crawled them on August 18. Four of those five were Spanish. Google says a page in this status "may or may not be indexed in the future." So keep the page live and linked instead of deleting it.

Why would Google index an English page but not its Spanish translation?

We measured that this happens on our site, but we can't prove why. Our best guesses: translated pages on a young site may look like near-copies of pages Google already stored (though Google's multi-language guide treats duplicate content as a same-language issue); almost nobody links to our Spanish pages from elsewhere on the web; and Google may spend less effort on the version it thinks fewer people need. Google hasn't confirmed any of that. What our data does show: discovery wasn't the problem. Google reached our Spanish pages nearly as often as the English ones (35 vs 36) and then kept fewer of them.

Does being in a sitemap guarantee Google will crawl a page?

No. Google says a sitemap "doesn't guarantee that all the items in your sitemap will be crawled and indexed." One article on our site had been in our sitemap since July 19, 2026 and was linked from the homepage and other articles. Thirty-two days later Google still hadn't crawled it once. My take, from our own logs: manually requesting indexing in Search Console is the fastest lever we've found for pages Google hasn't crawled yet.
